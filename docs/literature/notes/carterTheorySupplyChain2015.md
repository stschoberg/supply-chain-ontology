## Summary
Argues that supply chain management theory skipped a theory of what is being managed, the supply chain itself. Using conceptual theory building with a network and complex adaptive systems lens, it proposes six foundational premises about the structure and boundary of the supply chain.

## Key claims
- FP1: the supply chain is a network of nodes and links. A node is an establishment that is an agent able to make decisions and maximize its own gain; a link is a transaction consisting of the flow of materials, information, and/or finance (p. 90).
- FP2: the network operates as a complex adaptive system, where every agent grapples with the tension between control and emergence (p. 91).
- FP3: the supply chain is relative to a particular product and focal agent; "there is no overarching, absolute supply chain for an agent" (p. 91).
- FP4: it consists of a physical supply chain (nodes the product flows through) and a support supply chain (nodes it does not flow through, e.g. financial institutions, brokers, some carriers) (pp. 91-92).
- FP5: the supply chain is bounded by the visible horizon of the focal agent, meaning its knowledge of a node's existence, location, and activities (p. 93).
- FP6: that horizon is subject to attenuation with physical distance, cultural distance, and closeness centrality (p. 94).
- Most research and management practice covers the physical chain inside the visible horizon; the support chain and anything beyond the horizon are largely unexamined (p. 94).
- Limits acknowledged: physical products only (no services), consumer excluded from the definition of a node (p. 95).

## Method / data
Conceptual theory building; no empirical data. Suggests empirical work on supply chain evolution, life cycles, and the factors that shape the visible horizon (pp. 95-96).

## Relevance to our project
Defines links as flows of materials, information, and finance and bounds the supply chain by what a focal firm knows about other firms, so information visibility is part of the definition, which connects to our interoperability framing. It conflicts with Mentzer et al. (2001), who treat a supply chain as an absolute set of three or more entities that exists whether or not it is managed, where Carter et al. say it is always relative to a product and a focal agent. How we model that difference is a decision to make.

Boundary as a pragmatic engineering concern: the paper frames the boundary problem as a balance between realism and pragmatism, avoiding both an overly simple triad and a vast supplier network "that changes its shape constantly" (pp. 89, 93). Its answer is to bound the chain by the focal agent's visible horizon for a given product. For us this suggests scoping any model or graph to a focal firm and product instead of loading a whole supplier network. Whether that boundary should be a modeled concept or just a scoping decision is open.
