## Summary
Reviews the SCM literature, finds that "supply chain management" is used for three different things (a philosophy, the activities implementing it, and a set of processes), and separates them into Supply Chain Orientation (the philosophy) and Supply Chain Management (its cross-firm implementation). It defines "supply chain" itself, describes SCM's antecedents (trust, commitment, interdependence, compatibility, leadership) and consequences (lower cost, customer value, competitive advantage), and proposes one unified SCM definition and a conceptual model.

## Key claims
- A supply chain is "a set of three or more entities (organizations or individuals) directly involved in the upstream and downstream flows of products, services, finances, and/or information from a source to a customer" (p. 4), in three degrees: direct, extended, ultimate.
- Supply chains exist whether or not anyone manages them; SCM is the overt management of one (p. 4). One firm can sit in many supply chains, and the final consumer is a member.
- The SCM literature conflates two concepts. Supply Chain Orientation (SCO) is a firm's recognition of the systemic, strategic implications of managing the flows; SCM is the implementation of SCO across suppliers and customers, which requires several directly linked firms to have SCO (p. 11).
- Four flows are central: products/services, finances, information, plus demand and forecast information (p. 18).
- Unified definition: SCM is "the systemic, strategic coordination of the traditional business functions and the tactics across these business functions within a particular company and across businesses within the supply chain, for the purposes of improving the long-term performance of the individual companies and the supply chain as a whole" (pp. 17-18).
- SCM is broader than logistics: logistics is one function within it, alongside marketing, sales, R&D, finance, and others (pp. 16-17).
- Organizational scope: SCM spans three or more companies, usually organized as a network with a leader firm (p. 17).

## Relevance to our project
Working framing (hypothesis, not a conclusion): treat SCO and SCM as cross-firm activities, and tie the information problem to interoperability of data systems. A supply chain needs three or more firms (p. 4), SCM needs several directly linked firms sharing an orientation (p. 11), and information sharing is a core activity (Table 2, p. 8). The paper says nothing about data systems, so the interoperability link has to come from other sources.

Candidate scenario: a direct supply chain (supplier, manufacturer, customer) with independently built data models, tested on a cross-firm competency question. This gives BFO vs. non-BFO approaches a concrete criterion to be compared on, and it should be able to come out against BFO, not only for it.

Example: a leader firm provides a BFO-based ontology as the shared vocabulary that coordinates SCO across the other firms. This fits the paper's supply chain leader / "channel captain" role (p. 14). Mentzer also warns that forced participation by a strong leader encourages exit where firms have the option (p. 14), which is a cost to weigh against what the shared ontology buys.
